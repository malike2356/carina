# 89 - Small-Scale Commercial-to-Residential Property Development

## Purpose
A complete guide to small-scale commercial-to-residential property development in the UK. Covers permitted development rights, deal stacking, funding, professional team, exit strategies, and typical profit margins. Written for Carina to explain development to investors considering this strategy.

---

## 1. What Is Small-Scale Property Development?

Small-scale property development means converting existing commercial or mixed-use buildings into residential units. It is not:
- Building large housing estates from scratch
- Multi-storey tower blocks
- Ground-up construction on bare land (though that is also a form of development)

It is:
- Converting an old pub into 4 flats
- Turning a redundant office block into apartments
- Converting a Gospel Hall or old bank into 6-10 residential units
- Taking a derelict shop and creating two or three homes above the ground floor

These projects typically produce 4-20 residential units and can be completed in 12-30 months.

## 2. Permitted Development Rights (PDR)

Many commercial-to-residential conversions do not require full planning permission. Instead they use **Permitted Development Rights** - a legal shortcut introduced and expanded by the government to address the housing shortage.

**Class MA (Commercial to Residential):** Covers Class E commercial buildings (shops, offices, restaurants, gyms, banks, cafes) converting to residential (C3). Introduced in 2021, replacing the old office-to-residential PDR. Key conditions:
- Building must have been commercial for at least 2 years prior to application
- Maximum 1,500 sqm of floorspace can be converted under PDR per application
- Must go through Prior Approval process (not full planning, but council checks flood risk, transport, contamination, natural light, noise)
- Cannot be used in some conservation areas or Article 4 Directions (areas where councils have specifically removed PDR to protect character)

**Class Q (Agricultural to Residential):** Farm buildings to residential. Separate rules apply.

**Class P (Storage/Distribution to Residential):** B8 warehouses to homes.

**Why PDR matters for investors:**
- Faster than full planning (8 weeks for Prior Approval vs 8-13 weeks for planning, often longer in practice)
- More certain outcome (less likely to be rejected on design/policy grounds)
- Lower cost (architect fees for Prior Approval are lower)
- Enables deals that would be commercially unviable with full planning risk

**Article 4 Directions:** Local councils can remove PDR in specific areas. Always check with the council planning department before assuming PDR applies. Article 4 areas typically cover town centres, conservation areas, and high-demand residential zones where councils want to protect commercial premises.

## 3. The Developer's Role - Acting as CEO

The most important mental shift for new developers is understanding the role you play. You are the CEO - not the builder, architect, plumber, or project manager.

**What the CEO does:**
- Identifies the opportunity (the building, the deal)
- Assembles the professional team
- Secures the funding
- Sets the vision and specification
- Makes key decisions (how many units, what spec, what exit)
- Manages the project manager and monitors progress
- Manages the exit (marketing for sale or refinancing to hold)

**What the CEO does NOT do:**
- Lay foundations
- Wire electrics
- Install plumbing
- Design the architectural plans (unless qualified)
- Project-manage day-to-day on site

The professional team does the work. Your job is to direct the team, not be on the tools.

## 4. The Professional Team for Small-Scale Development

A typical team for a commercial-to-residential conversion of 5-15 units:

| Professional | Role | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Architect | Design, planning/prior approval drawings, planning conditions | £8,000-£25,000 depending on scheme size |
| Structural engineer | Foundation design, structural calculations | £3,000-£8,000 |
| Project manager | Day-to-day site management, contractor coordination | 5-8% of construction costs |
| Main contractor | Construction, manages sub-trades | Quoted per build |
| Planning consultant | PDR strategy, Article 4 advice, dealing with council | £2,000-£5,000 |
| Building control surveyor | Compliance with Building Regulations | £1,500-£4,000 |
| Commercial funding broker | Securing development finance | Fee or commission from lender |
| Solicitor | Purchase, corporate, leasehold creation on new units | £3,000-£8,000 |
| QS (Quantity Surveyor) | Cost monitoring (required by many development lenders) | £3,000-£6,000 |
| Selling agent or lettings agent | Exit strategy execution | Sales: 1-2%; Letting: 10-15% monthly |

**You do not need to be qualified in any of these disciplines.** The professionals you hire carry those qualifications. Your value is finding the deal, directing the vision, and holding the team accountable.

## 5. Identifying a Good Development Deal

Target building characteristics:
- Commercial or mixed-use building that has been vacant or underused
- Motivated seller (estate sale, relocating business, financial difficulty)
- In a residential area or on the fringe of town centre (demand for housing is clear)
- Structurally sound enough that conversion is viable (check roof, walls, foundations)
- Eligible for PDR or likely to receive planning permission
- Multiple units possible from the floorspace (to spread fixed costs over more units)

**The "Need Market" principle:**
Target 1-bedroom, 2-bedroom, and 3-bedroom units. These are the need market - people have to live somewhere, regardless of whether they are buying or renting. 4+ bedroom homes are the want market - discretionary and sensitive to economic conditions. Sticking to 1-3 bed units gives you a wider buyer/tenant pool and reduces unsold inventory risk.

**Adding value through design:**
Look for hidden floorspace others miss - roof spaces, basement areas, outbuildings. A building other developers dismissed because the numbers seemed marginal may work if you see a 3-bed apartment in the roof space that everyone else ignored.

## 6. Stacking a Development Deal

Stacking means running the numbers before committing any money to see if the deal is viable.

**Key inputs:**
- Purchase price of the building
- Development costs (build/conversion costs, professional fees, finance costs, contingency)
- GDV - Gross Development Value (what all completed units will be worth or rent for)
- Finance arrangement

**Key formula:**
```
Profit = GDV - Purchase Price - Development Costs - Finance Costs - Sales/Marketing Costs
Gross Development Margin = Profit / GDV × 100
```

**Typical viable margin:**
- Minimum 20% of GDV (development lenders want to see this for security)
- Target 25-30% of GDV for a good deal
- 15% or below is too thin unless there is exceptional certainty of GDV

**Example - 7 unit commercial conversion:**
- Purchase price: £180,000
- Development costs: £420,000 (£60,000 per unit average for fit-out, services, communal areas)
- Professional fees and finance costs: £80,000
- Total cost: £680,000
- GDV at completion: 7 units × £130,000 average = £910,000
- Sales costs (2%): £18,200
- Net profit: £910,000 - £680,000 - £18,200 = £211,800
- Gross margin: £211,800 / £910,000 = 23.3%

## 7. Development Finance

Development finance is a specialist product, different from a buy-to-let or residential mortgage. It is deal-based, not income-based.

**What lenders assess (in order of priority):**
1. The deal itself - does the GDV, costs, and margin make sense?
2. The professional team - experience of the project manager and contractors
3. Two exit strategies - what happens if the primary exit fails?
4. Developer's experience - leaned against by the team's experience for new developers
5. Developer's equity input and assets/liabilities

**Note:** Your personal income, salary, or payslips are largely irrelevant for development finance. A first-time developer with zero personal property can get development finance if the deal stacks and the team is credible.

**How development finance is structured:**
- Lender provides a facility (e.g., £600,000 agreed maximum)
- Funds drawn down in tranches as build progresses (after QS sign-off)
- Interest charged on drawn funds only (not the full facility)
- Exit: repay on sale of units, or refinance onto BTL/commercial mortgage if holding

**Typical terms:**
- Loan-to-cost (LTC): 70-80% of total project costs
- Loan-to-GDV: 60-65% of GDV
- Interest: 0.8-1.3% per month (10-16% annually)
- Arrangement fee: 1.5-2%
- Monitoring surveyor (QS) required: lender appoints and you pay
- Facility term: 12-24 months

**Getting funding as a new developer:**
Use a specialist commercial funding broker, not a high street bank. High street banks do not do development finance. Tell the broker what you are looking to do, the types of deals and numbers. They will match you to lenders. The broker does the heavy lifting on the funding process - the same way your project manager does the heavy lifting on-site.

## 8. Exit Strategies

Always have a minimum of two exits planned before starting.

### Exit 1: Sell the completed units
- Market the completed units (houses, flats) for sale on the open market
- Target the need market (1-3 bed) for broadest buyer appeal
- Developer receives cash profit, repays development finance
- Clean and simple; best in a rising or stable market

### Exit 2: Refinance and hold (rent out)
- On completion, refinance onto BTL or commercial mortgage
- Rent the units out (single let, HMO, or serviced accommodation)
- Development loan repaid from refinanced mortgage proceeds
- Developer retains the asset and receives ongoing rental income
- Used when sale market conditions are unfavourable or developer wants a portfolio

### Exit 3: Specialist supported housing (guaranteed exit)
A guaranteed exit signed before the build starts. The developer identifies a specialist housing provider (organisations that house vulnerable adults, people with learning difficulties, or physical disabilities). They specify exactly what they need; the developer builds to that specification. A commercial full-repairing and insuring (FRI) lease is agreed - typically 10, 15, or 20 years - at an agreed rent.

**Why this matters:**
- The exit is locked in before the foundations are laid
- Lease provides guaranteed income for the lease term
- Suitable for conversions where uncertainty about the sales market is a concern
- Slightly longer front-end lead time (specification agreement, lease negotiation)
- Government-backed housing need means demand is high and relatively uncorrelated with general property market

## 9. Typical Deal Timeline

| Phase | Duration |
|---|---|
| Deal finding and analysis | 4-12 weeks |
| Legal purchase | 6-10 weeks |
| Prior Approval / Planning | 8-13 weeks (can overlap with purchase) |
| Design and tender | 4-8 weeks |
| Construction / conversion | 12-30 weeks depending on scheme size |
| Practical completion and snagging | 2-4 weeks |
| Sale or refinance exit | 4-12 weeks |
| **Total project duration** | **9-24 months** |

## 10. Why Development Beats Buy-to-Let on Margin

A typical BTL property generates:
- Net cash-on-cash return of 4-6% per year
- Capital growth of 3-5% per year (market dependent)

A development deal generates:
- 20-30% profit on GDV in a single project
- Capital recycled into the next deal (or refinanced and held)

On a comparable capital outlay, development generates significantly higher returns in compressed timeframes. The trade-off: more complexity, more risk if the deal is not stacked correctly, and more hands-on project involvement compared to a passive BTL investment.

Development is not passive investing - it is a business. If you want truly passive income, BTL or commercial property is more appropriate. If you want to maximize capital returns and build substantial wealth faster, development is the path.

## 11. Common First-Timer Mistakes

- Not checking for Article 4 Directions before committing to purchase
- Underestimating development costs (no contingency budget - always add 15-20%)
- Only planning one exit
- Using a residential mortgage broker instead of a specialist development finance broker
- Not assembling a credible professional team before approaching lenders
- Focusing on the want market (luxury units, 4+ beds) instead of the need market
- Choosing the cheapest contractors without references and written contracts
- Not having a project manager - trying to manage the contractors yourself while working full-time
