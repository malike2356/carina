# 88 - UK Property Finance for Investors

## Purpose
A complete reference on every financing route available to UK property investors. Covers buy-to-let mortgages, bridging finance, HMO mortgages, commercial mortgages, development finance, joint ventures, equity release, and no-money-down strategies with their real risks. Written for Carina to explain finance clearly to beginners.

---

## 1. Buy-to-Let (BTL) Mortgages

### What they are
A BTL mortgage is specifically for properties you intend to let to tenants, not live in. They are regulated differently from residential mortgages, have higher rates, and require larger deposits.

### Key criteria lenders use

**Minimum deposit:** 25% of the property value (75% LTV maximum). Some specialist lenders go to 80% LTV but at significantly higher rates.

**Rental income stress test:** This is the key differentiator between BTL and residential mortgages. Lenders do not primarily assess your personal income - they assess whether the rent will cover the mortgage payment by a comfortable margin. Most lenders require:
- Rent must cover 125% of the monthly mortgage payment (at a stressed rate, typically 5.5-7%, not the actual rate)
- Example: mortgage payment at stress rate is £600/mo. Minimum rent required = £600 × 1.25 = £750/mo

**Your personal income:** Some lenders have no minimum income requirement. Many require £25,000/yr. A few require more, especially for portfolio landlords.

**Portfolio landlords:** If you own 4 or more mortgaged properties, most high street lenders classify you as a "portfolio landlord" and apply stricter portfolio-wide stress tests. Specialist BTL lenders (including Precise Mortgages, Paragon, Fleet, Foundation Home Loans) are more accustomed to portfolio landlords.

**Credit history:** Most BTL lenders require a clean credit file. Some specialist lenders will accept satisfied defaults or CCJs over 3 years old. Bankruptcy within the last 6 years is typically excluded.

**Property type restrictions:** Some lenders will not lend on ex-council properties, properties above commercial premises, properties over a certain number of storeys, studio flats under 30m², or leasehold properties with under 70-80 years remaining.

### Interest-only vs repayment

**Interest-only BTL mortgage:** You pay only the interest on the loan each month. The capital balance never reduces. Monthly payments are lower, improving cash flow. At the end of the term (typically 25 years), the full loan amount is still outstanding and must be repaid - typically by selling the property.

Example: £120,000 IO mortgage at 5.5% = £550/mo payment. After 25 years the balance is still £120,000.

**Repayment BTL mortgage:** You pay interest plus a portion of capital each month. Monthly payments are higher, reducing cash flow. At the end of the term, the property is owned outright.

Example: £120,000 repayment mortgage at 5.5% over 25 years = £734/mo. After 25 years: zero balance.

**Most investors use interest-only** to maximise monthly cash flow and access equity via refinancing rather than repayment.

### Fixed vs tracker vs variable

**Fixed rate:** Payment is guaranteed for the fixed period (typically 2 or 5 years). Protection against rate rises. Arrangement fees can be higher. Early repayment charges (ERCs) apply if you exit before the fixed period ends.

**Tracker rate:** Follows Bank of England base rate + a margin. Payments move with base rate. Usually lower in stable rate environments. No ERCs on most trackers. Higher risk in rising rate environments.

**Variable/SVR:** The lender's standard rate after a fixed/tracker period ends. Always revert to a new product before this kicks in - SVRs are almost always the most expensive option.

### Typical costs

- Arrangement fee: £0-£2,000 (some products charge a percentage of the loan instead - e.g. 1.5%)
- Valuation fee: £150-£600 (lender instructs this, you pay)
- Broker fee: £0-£500 (many specialist brokers charge a fee)
- Legal fees: £300-£500 (lender's solicitor costs, separate from your conveyancer)
- Exit/redemption fee: £50-£300 when you repay/remortgage

### The remortgage cycle

Most BTL investors remortgage every 2-5 years to:
1. Access a new lower rate (avoiding SVR)
2. Release equity for deposits on new properties (if property value has risen)
3. Raise capital for refurb or business purposes

Example: bought for £150,000, now worth £200,000. Outstanding mortgage: £112,500 (75% of original value). Remortgage at 75% of new value = £150,000. Released equity = £150,000 - £112,500 = **£37,500 cash** (minus arrangement fees and legal costs). This cash can be deployed as a deposit on the next deal.

## 2. HMO Mortgages

HMO mortgages are a specialist product for properties let to multiple unrelated tenants. Not all BTL lenders offer them.

**Key differences from standard BTL:**
- Higher minimum deposit: typically 25-30% (some lenders require 35%)
- Higher interest rates: 0.3-0.8% above standard BTL
- Room-by-room rental income used in stress test (not just the aggregate)
- Lenders want the property to be already licensed or licensable
- Some lenders cap the number of rooms (maximum 6 or 8 rooms)
- Specialist HMO lenders: Paragon, Precise, Foundation, Shawbrook, Aldermore

**For large HMOs (7+ rooms) or purpose-built HMO blocks:** Commercial or semi-commercial mortgages may be required.

## 3. Bridging Finance

### What it is
A short-term, high-interest loan designed to "bridge" a gap - typically used when you need to complete quickly, the property is unmortgageable in its current state (needs refurb), or you need cash while waiting for another property to sell.

### When investors use it
- **BRR strategy:** Buy a distressed property that no BTL lender will touch, refurb it, refinance onto a BTL mortgage once habitable
- **Auction purchases:** Auction legal completion is typically 28 days - too fast for a standard mortgage application. Bridge, then refinance.
- **Development land or complex deals:** Commercial conversions, planning gain purchases
- **Short-term equity release:** Accessing cash quickly from an existing property (though an equity release mortgage is usually better long-term)

### How bridging finance works

You borrow against the property's current value (or "gross development value" on refurb projects). Lenders typically lend:
- Up to 75% LTV on the current value (day 1 value)
- Some lenders lend against "end value" (the estimated value after works) at up to 70%

Interest is charged monthly, typically at 0.5-1.5% per month (6-18% per year). This is significantly higher than a BTL mortgage.

**Interest options:**
- **Retained interest:** The entire term's interest is deducted from the loan upfront. You receive less cash but have no monthly payments. Best for projects where you have no other income to service the loan.
- **Serviced interest:** You pay interest monthly. You receive the full loan but must have cash flow to service it. Better if you have rental income coming in.
- **Rolled interest:** Interest accumulates and is added to the loan balance. Paid in full at exit. No monthly payments.

### Bridging loan example (BRR)

Purchase price: £65,000
Refurb cost: £20,000
End (post-refurb) value: £110,000

Bridging loan at 70% of end value = £77,000
This covers purchase (£65,000) + some refurb costs. You put in the remaining £8,000 + fees.

Cost: 1% per month over 6 months on £77,000 = £4,620 interest

After refurb, refinance onto BTL mortgage at 75% LTV of £110,000 = £82,500 BTL mortgage
Repay bridging loan (£77,000 + £4,620 interest) = £81,620
Capital returned: £82,500 - £81,620 = **£880 left over**
You own a £110,000 property with a £82,500 BTL mortgage and have recovered nearly all your capital.

### Key bridging terms

**Open bridge:** No fixed exit date. Flexible but slightly more expensive.
**Closed bridge:** Fixed exit date agreed. Cheaper but requires certainty (e.g. contracts already exchanged on sale).
**First charge bridge:** Lender holds first charge on the property. Standard.
**Second charge bridge:** Lender takes second charge behind an existing mortgage. Rare, expensive.
**Exit strategy:** The lender will demand to know your exit plan (how you will repay the bridge) before lending. Always have a clear exit - the most common exits are refinance onto BTL or sale of property.

## 4. Commercial Mortgages

For offices, retail units, industrial premises, mixed-use (flat over shop), and large HMO blocks.

**Key differences:**
- Minimum deposit: 30-40% (60-70% LTV maximum)
- Interest rates: higher than residential BTL (typically 2-4% above base rate)
- Shorter terms: often 10-20 years (not 25-year terms standard in residential)
- Assessed on the commercial income (rent from the business tenant), not the owner's personal income
- Often variable rate or short fixed terms (2-3 years)

**Semi-commercial (mixed use):** A property with both residential and commercial elements (e.g. flat over shop) is assessed partly on commercial criteria. LTVs are typically lower than pure residential.

## 5. Development Finance

For ground-up construction, barn conversions, large commercial-to-residential conversions, or major refurbs going beyond habitable into structural.

**How it works:**
- Lend against land value and build costs
- Funds drawn down in stages as build progresses (not released all upfront)
- Monitored by a project monitoring surveyor
- Exit: sell the completed units or refinance onto BTL/commercial mortgage
- Rates: 1-1.5% per month (12-18% annualised), often with arrangement fees of 1.5-2%

**Criteria:**
- Experience: many lenders require a track record. First-time developers often need a joint venture or experienced professional on the team.
- Professional team: architect, structural engineer, QS (quantity surveyor) required
- Planning: outline or full planning permission must already be granted

## 6. Joint Ventures (JVs)

A JV is a contractual partnership between two or more parties for a single deal, not an ongoing business relationship (that would be a partnership or LLP).

### Why investors use JVs
- **You have the skill but not the capital:** You find and manage the deal; a money partner provides the funds.
- **You have the capital but not the time:** A deal sourcer or active investor runs the deal; you provide the money.
- **The deal is too large for one person:** Pooling capital to buy larger assets.

### Common JV structures

**50/50 profit split:** Each party contributes equally (or as agreed) and splits returns equally.
**Capital preference return:** The money partner gets their capital back first plus a preferred return (e.g. 8% p.a. on invested capital) before profits are split.
**Equity split:** One party takes a larger equity share in proportion to contribution.

### Essential JV documentation

A properly drafted JV agreement (from a commercial solicitor) should specify:
- Each party's contribution (cash, time, expertise, property)
- Decision-making authority (who can sign contracts, approve costs)
- Profit distribution mechanics and timing
- What happens if one party wants to exit
- What happens on death or incapacity of either party
- Dispute resolution

**Never enter a JV without a written agreement.** Verbal JVs regularly end in costly litigation.

### JV legal structure options

**Unincorporated JV:** Simple contract between individuals. Tax transparent (each pays tax on their share). Simple but less protection.
**SPV (Special Purpose Vehicle):** A new limited company formed specifically for the deal. Each party holds shares. More setup cost (£300-£500) but cleaner separation of assets. Used for larger deals.

## 7. No-Money-Down Strategies (and what they really mean)

"No money down" is a popular marketing term in property education. It is real - experienced investors do structure deals requiring zero or minimal personal capital. But there are always costs, risks, and prerequisites that beginners are not always told about.

### Legitimate no-money-down approaches

**Vendor finance (seller financing):**
The vendor agrees to accept payments over time rather than a full lump sum at completion. Rare in the UK residential market. More common in commercial or during motivated seller situations. Requires a vendor willing to act as lender.

**100% bridging:**
Some bridging lenders will lend 100% of purchase price against a property with significant equity already in another asset (cross-charge). You borrow against property A to buy property B. You need an existing property with equity. Total debt increases.

**Refinancing existing equity:**
You remortgage a property you already own to release equity, then use that equity as the deposit for a new purchase. No new capital needed - but you increase your total debt. This works when property values have risen.

**R2R (Rent to Rent):**
As described in knowledge file 86. Not zero cost (£2,000-£8,000 for setup, deposit, first month's rent) but far below property purchase costs. The property remains owned by the landlord.

**Below Market Value (BMV) purchase + bridging:**
If you buy a property significantly below market value (e.g. £80,000 worth £120,000), a bridging lender may lend 70% of the market value (£84,000) - more than the purchase price. Net result: you receive cash back at the point of purchase. Only works with genuine BMV deals. You still need the bridging costs.

**Joint venture with a money partner:**
Partner provides 100% of the capital; you source, manage, and run the deal; profits split. No capital needed from you, but you must deliver results.

### What "no money down" always costs

Even when no deposit changes hands:
- Legal fees: £800-£2,000
- Bridging fees and interest (if bridging used): £3,000-£8,000+
- Time and expertise (your labour has a value)
- Risk: if the deal fails to perform, you still owe the lender or your JV partner

### The real prerequisite for true no-money-down deals

You need either:
1. Existing property equity to refinance, OR
2. A money partner who trusts you, OR
3. Exceptional deal-finding skills to buy so far below market value that lender security covers the purchase price

Beginners without track record or existing assets will almost always need some capital. The honest path is to start with your own capital (however small - R2R, rent-to-SA) and grow from there.

## 8. Equity Release and Further Advances

### Further advance (from your current lender)
If you already have a BTL mortgage and your property has increased in value, you can ask the same lender for a "further advance" - additional borrowing secured against the same property. This is faster than a full remortgage (no legal fees for a new mortgage, sometimes no full valuation).

Limitations: the lender only goes to their maximum LTV (usually 75% for BTL). The rate on the further advance may differ from your existing mortgage rate.

### Remortgage to release equity
Remortgage the whole property to a new lender at the new higher valuation. Full new mortgage = higher loan amount. Release the difference as cash. Full legal fees and product fees apply, but often gives access to better rates and higher loan amounts.

### Second charge mortgage
A second mortgage taken against a property that already has a first mortgage. The second charge lender is in second position - if you default, the first charge lender gets paid first. Second charges are more expensive and riskier. Used when you cannot remortgage the first charge (e.g. in a fixed rate period with high ERCs).

## 9. Stress testing your deal before applying

Before approaching a lender or broker, run these checks:

**Gross yield check:**
(Annual rent / purchase price) × 100 = gross yield
Minimum 7% for BTL to be financeable in most market conditions.

**Lender stress test:**
Monthly rent × 12 / 12 × 0.8 (80% to reflect voids and costs) / 1.25 (DSCR requirement) = maximum mortgage payment
Maximum mortgage payment × 12 / mortgage rate (use 5.5%) = rough maximum loan amount

Example: rent £900/mo. Stressed rent = £900 × 0.8 = £720. DSCR-adjusted = £720 / 1.25 = £576 max payment. At 5.5% IO: £576 × 12 / 0.055 = £125,673 max loan.

**Cash flow check:**
Monthly rent - mortgage - management (10%) - maintenance (5%) - insurance (£25) = monthly profit
If negative: reconsider price, negotiate lower, or choose a different property.

**Total capital required:**
Deposit + SDLT + legal fees + survey + arrangement fees + refurb + compliance certificates + first month void = cash out of pocket before first rent cheque.

## 10. Limited company (Ltd) vs personal ownership

### The tax driver

Under Section 24 (introduced 2017, fully phased in by 2020), personal landlords can no longer deduct mortgage interest from rental income as a cost. Instead they receive a 20% tax credit on the interest paid.

For basic rate (20%) taxpayers: same effective outcome.
For higher rate (40%) taxpayers: significant disadvantage. On a £12,000/yr interest payment, the 20% credit = £2,400 relief instead of the £4,800 they would have received before Section 24.

### Limited company benefits
- Mortgage interest fully deductible as a business expense (corporation tax only)
- Corporation tax rate: 19-25% (smaller than 40% income tax for higher earners)
- Retained profits reinvested within the company grow faster
- Easier to involve family members as shareholders (income splitting)

### Limited company drawbacks
- Higher mortgage rates: specialist BTL lenders charge 0.5-1% higher for limited company BTL
- Accountancy costs: a limited company requires annual accounts, confirmation statement, corporation tax return. Add £800-£2,500/yr in accountancy fees.
- Extracting money: profits in the company must be extracted as salary or dividends - both taxable. Not accessible like a personal bank account.
- Capital gains on transfer: if you already own property personally and want to move it into a company, you trigger capital gains tax and SDLT on the transfer (as if you sold to yourself at market value). Expensive.

### The practical guidance

For beginners buying their first 1-2 properties: assess your personal tax bracket. If you are a basic rate taxpayer, buy personally (simpler). If you are a 40% taxpayer with significant earned income, buy in a limited company from the start.

For anyone already holding 3+ properties personally: speak to a property tax specialist before doing anything else. The decision to incorporate (or not) is complex and highly individual.

## 11. Working with a specialist mortgage broker

Do not use a high street bank or a residential mortgage broker for investment property. Use a whole-of-market broker who specialises specifically in:
- Buy-to-let
- HMO mortgages
- Bridging and development finance
- Portfolio landlords
- Limited company BTL

A good specialist broker knows which lenders will accept your situation, which products have the most favourable stress test calculations, and which lenders have the shortest completion times. They will save you more money than their fee costs.

Questions to ask a broker:
1. Are you whole-of-market (access to all lenders, not just a panel)?
2. Do you specialise in investment property?
3. What is your fee structure?
4. Have you arranged mortgages for portfolio landlords with limited companies?
5. Which lenders do you use most often for HMO or bridging?

Broker fee: £0-£500 typically. Some charge a percentage of the loan (0.3-1%). Clarify before engaging.
