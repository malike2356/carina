# EPC Requirements for Landlords: What You Need to Know in 2026

Energy Performance Certificate (EPC) rules have been a fact of life for landlords for over a decade, but the requirements are changing. The government is pushing towards higher minimum standards, and if you own rental property, you need to know what is coming.

This guide covers the current rules, the proposed changes, and what you should be doing to prepare.

## Current minimum standard

Since April 2020, it has been illegal to let a property in England and Wales with an EPC rating below E. The fine for non-compliance is up to GBP 5,000, and if you have not lodged an EPC for a property you are letting, you could face a separate penalty of GBP 200.

The current rules apply to:

- New tenancies granted on or after 1 April 2020
- All existing tenancies since 1 April 2020
- Renewals and statutory periodic tenancies

There are limited exemptions. If you have carried out all cost-effective improvements but the property still cannot reach an E rating, you can register an exemption on the PRS Exemptions Register. Exemptions last five years and must be renewed if the property still cannot meet the standard.

## What is coming: Minimum EPC C

The government has been consulting on raising the minimum standard to EPC C for new tenancies since 2025, with all existing tenancies required to comply by 2028. While the timeline has shifted over the years, the direction of travel is clear. A minimum EPC C is coming.

For investors, this matters because a significant proportion of the UK's private rented stock is currently rated D or below. According to government data, roughly 60% of rented properties already meet EPC C or above, but that still leaves around 40% that will need upgrades.

In Hampshire, where a large proportion of the housing stock is Victorian and Edwardian terraces in cities like Portsmouth and Southampton, the proportion of properties below C is likely higher than the national average.

## What improvements actually help

The EPC assessment looks at several factors. The most impactful improvements for older properties are:

- **Loft insulation:** One of the cheapest and most effective improvements. Topping up loft insulation to 270mm can significantly improve the rating for minimal cost (GBP 300 to GBP 500).
- **Cavity wall insulation:** If the property has cavity walls, filling them costs GBP 500 to GBP 1,500 and can improve the rating by several bands.
- **Solid wall insulation:** More expensive (GBP 5,000 to GBP 20,000 depending on internal or external) but can transform the EPC of an older property.
- **Double glazing:** If a property still has single glazing, replacing it with double glazing can make a meaningful difference. Costs vary significantly by property size.
- **Condensing boiler replacement:** An old non-condensing boiler is a major drag on the EPC. Replacing it with a modern condensing boiler plus full heating controls is often one of the most cost-effective improvements.
- **LED lighting:** Replacing all bulbs with LEDs is cheap and can contribute a few points to the rating.
- **Solar panels:** Expensive (GBP 5,000 to GBP 8,000) but can push a D or E rating up to C or above. Also adds value to the property and reduces tenant energy bills.

The order matters. Start with the cheapest improvements, get the EPC reassessed, and only invest in expensive measures if you have to.

## Planning for the minimum C standard

If you own rental properties that are currently rated D or E, here is what to do:

1. Check your current EPC. If you do not have one, get one done. They are valid for ten years.
2. Look at the recommendation report that comes with the EPC. It tells you exactly what improvements would increase the rating.
3. Start with the low-cost improvements. Most D-rated properties can reach C with loft insulation, cavity wall insulation, and heating upgrades.
4. Budget for the work. A property moving from E to C might cost GBP 3,000 to GBP 10,000 depending on the starting point and the property type.
5. Plan the timing. If you have a tenancy ending soon, use the void period to carry out improvements.

## EPC exemptions

If you have done all cost-effective improvements and the property still cannot reach the required standard, you can register an exemption. However, the exemption process is not a simple opt-out. You must:

- Have a valid EPC that shows the rating
- Have a report from an assessor or surveyor confirming that all relevant improvements have been made or are not feasible
- Register the exemption on the national register

False exemptions carry the same penalties as non-compliance.

## What about listed buildings

Listed buildings and properties in conservation areas may be exempt from some EPC requirements if the energy efficiency improvements would unacceptably alter the building's character. This is not a blanket exemption. You need to take professional advice on what improvements are feasible and what the legal position is.

## Final thought

The minimum EPC C standard is coming. The exact timeline may shift, but the direction of travel is unambiguous. Properties that cannot reach a C rating will eventually become unlettable, which will affect both rental income and capital values.

The sensible approach is to act now, not when the deadline arrives. Carry out the cost-effective improvements, budget for the bigger ones, and make sure your portfolio is positioned for the new standard well before it becomes mandatory.
