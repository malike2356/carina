# Property Tax Planning: Strategies for UK Investors

Tax is one of the largest costs of owning rental property, and the rules have changed significantly over the last decade. Understanding how to structure your property investments tax-efficiently can make the difference between a good return and a mediocre one.

This guide covers the main tax considerations for property investors in 2026.

## Stamp Duty Land Tax on additional properties

When you buy a residential property in England, you pay Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT). The rates are tiered: no SDLT on the first GBP 250,000, 5% on the portion from GBP 250,001 to GBP 925,000, and so on.

However, if you already own a property and buy an additional one, you pay an extra 3% surcharge on each band. This means a buy-to-let investor purchasing a GBP 300,000 property pays SDLT of GBP 13,500, compared to GBP 2,500 for a first-time buyer.

There is also an additional 2% surcharge for non-UK residents buying residential property in England and Northern Ireland, which took effect from April 2021.

The surcharge does not apply to commercial property transactions. If you buy a mixed-use property (commercial with residential above), the residential surcharge may apply to the residential element only, depending on the split. Professional advice is essential here.

## Income tax on rental profits

Your net rental income (rent received minus allowable expenses) is added to your other income and taxed at your marginal rate: 20% for basic rate, 40% for higher rate, or 45% for additional rate taxpayers.

Allowable expenses include:

- Mortgage interest (see below for the restriction)
- Letting agent fees and management charges
- Repairs and maintenance (but not improvements or capital works)
- Insurance
- Council tax when the property is empty
- Ground rent and service charges
- Legal fees for renewing a tenancy (but not for buying the property)
- Accountancy fees
- Travel costs directly related to the property (within reason)
- Phone and internet costs (apportioned for the property)

A common mistake is confusing repairs with improvements. Repairs (fixing a leaking roof) are allowable. Improvements (adding a conservatory) are capital expenditure and are not allowed against income tax. They are added to the cost base for capital gains purposes.

## Mortgage interest restriction

Since April 2020, landlords cannot deduct mortgage interest from rental income before calculating tax. Instead, you receive a 20% tax credit based on your mortgage interest.

This means higher rate and additional rate taxpayers cannot claim full relief on their mortgage costs. A higher rate landlord with GBP 10,000 of mortgage interest gets a GBP 2,000 tax credit instead of GBP 4,000 of relief under the old system.

The practical effect is that leveraged landlords pay more tax. It makes calculating net returns before buying more important than ever, especially when financing costs are high.

The restriction only applies to residential properties. Furnished holiday lets (qualifying as a business) can still deduct mortgage interest in full.

## Furnished holiday lettings

Qualifying furnished holiday lets (FHLs) are treated more favourably for tax purposes:

- Mortgage interest can be deducted from income as a business expense
- Profits count as earnings for pension contribution purposes
- Capital gains tax reliefs (Business Asset Disposal Relief, Rollover Relief) are available
- Capital allowances are available on furniture and equipment (standard residential lets cannot claim these)

To qualify as an FHL, the property must be:

- Furnished
- Available for letting for at least 210 days per year
- Actually let for at least 105 days per year
- Not occupied by the same person for more than 31 continuous days for more than 155 days per year

The FHL rules are currently under review by the government. The favourable tax treatment may change in future, but for now, qualifying properties benefit from significant advantages.

## Capital gains tax

When you sell a property that is not your main home, you pay capital gains tax on the profit above your annual allowance (GBP 3,000 for individuals in 2026/27).

The rates are:

- 18% for basic rate taxpayers (on gains within the basic rate band)
- 24% for higher and additional rate taxpayers

You must report and pay CGT within 60 days of completion on residential property disposals. This is a strict deadline. Missing it incurs interest and penalties.

## Using a company structure

Many landlords have incorporated their portfolios since the mortgage interest restriction came in. A limited company pays corporation tax (currently 19% to 25% depending on profit level) on rental profits rather than income tax, and can deduct mortgage interest in full.

The trade-offs are:

- Higher setup and running costs (company accounts, tax returns)
- Paying tax again when extracting profits (dividends or salary)
- Higher SDLT for purchasing through a company
- Harder to get buy-to-let mortgages in a company name
- Potential constraints on property sales later

Incorporation is not right for everyone. For a small portfolio, the costs outweigh the benefits. For a growing portfolio with significant leverage, the tax savings can be substantial.

## Inheritance tax

Property is part of your estate for inheritance tax purposes. The nil-rate band is GBP 325,000, with an additional residence nil-rate band of GBP 175,000 when passing a main home to direct descendants.

Rental properties do not qualify for the residence nil-rate band. If your estate exceeds the nil-rate band, your heirs pay 40% on the excess.

Planning options include holding property in a company structure, transferring to a spouse, or using trusts. These are complex areas that require professional advice.

## Final thought

Tax planning for property is not about evasion. It is about understanding the rules and structuring your investments to pay no more than you have to. The rules change regularly, so what worked five years ago may not work today.
