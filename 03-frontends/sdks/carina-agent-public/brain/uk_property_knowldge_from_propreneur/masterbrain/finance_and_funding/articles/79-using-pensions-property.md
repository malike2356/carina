# Using Your Pension to Invest in Property

Your pension is one of the most tax-efficient ways to invest in property, but the rules are specific and getting them wrong can be expensive. This guide covers the main pension vehicles available for property investment and how they work.

## Self-Invested Personal Pensions (SIPPs)

A SIPP is a personal pension that gives you control over where your pension savings are invested. Unlike a standard pension where your money goes into a managed fund, a SIPP allows you to invest directly in assets including commercial property.

The SIPP trustees hold the legal title to the property, but you have the beneficial interest. The property is your pension asset.

### What you can buy with a SIPP

SIPPs can invest in:

- Commercial property (offices, shops, warehouses, industrial units)
- Land (though not residential land in most cases)
- Some types of mixed-use property (commercial with residential)

SIPPs cannot invest in:

- Residential property (since April 2006, residential property is a taxable asset in a SIPP)
- Property owned by you or a close family member (even commercial)
- Holiday lets (treated as residential for SIPP purposes)
- Residential ground rents

If you buy a property that does not qualify, you face a tax charge of up to 55% of the property value. The rules are strict.

### Financing purchases in a SIPP

You can use the funds in your SIPP to buy the property outright. You can also borrow to supplement the SIPP funds. The borrowing is limited to 50% of the SIPP's net value (the total pension fund minus any existing borrowing).

For example, if your SIPP is worth GBP 200,000, you can borrow up to GBP 100,000, giving you GBP 300,000 to invest in a property.

The borrowing must be on a commercial basis and limited-recourse (the lender cannot pursue your other SIPP assets if the borrowing defaults).

### Tax advantages of SIPP property investment

- Rental income is tax-free within the SIPP (no income tax)
- Capital gains on property sales are tax-free within the SIPP (no CGT)
- Corporation tax on rental profits is not applicable (the SIPP is a tax-exempt vehicle)
- VAT on commercial property purchases can be recovered in some cases

When you retire, you can draw the income from the SIPP as a pension, paying income tax at your marginal rate. You can also take 25% of the SIPP value as a tax-free lump sum, though this requires crystallising part of the pension.

### Costs of holding property in a SIPP

- Annual SIPP administration fees: GBP 200 to GBP 500
- Property transaction fees (purchase and sale): GBP 500 to GBP 2,000 each
- Valuation fees: GBP 200 to GBP 500 per valuation
- Stamp duty on purchase (applies as normal)
- Letting agent and management fees: as normal
- Insurance: must be arranged by the SIPP administrator

The annual fees mean that a SIPP property only makes sense if the investment is large enough to justify the costs. Most advisers suggest a minimum property value of GBP 100,000 to GBP 150,000.

## Small Self-Administered Schemes (SSAS)

A SSAS is a company pension scheme, typically set up by directors of a limited company. It is more flexible than a SIPP and can invest in a wider range of assets.

Key advantages of a SSAS:

- Can invest in residential property, including property occupied by the sponsoring company's directors or employees (with restrictions)
- Can lend to the sponsoring company (up to 50% of the fund value)
- More flexibility in borrowing arrangements
- Can hold multiple properties within the same scheme

SSAS schemes are more complex to set up and administer than SIPPs, requiring professional trustees and annual reports. They work best for company directors who want significant control over their pension investments and have a large enough fund (typically GBP 250,000+) to justify the costs.

## Property and your existing pension

If you already have a pension, you may be able to transfer some or all of it to a SIPP and then use the SIPP to invest in property. This is a significant decision with long-term implications.

Before doing this, consider:

- The transfer value of your existing pension (with defined benefit schemes, the transfer value may be much lower than the promised benefits)
- The costs of setting up and running a SIPP
- The liquidity of property as a pension asset (you cannot sell a property quickly if you need pension income)
- The diversification risk (having all your pension in one or two properties is risky)

## Using a limited company pension

A limited company can pay pension contributions on behalf of its directors into a SIPP or SSAS. These contributions are tax-deductible for the company (reducing corporation tax) and do not attract National Insurance.

A company director who owns rental properties through a limited company can channeller pension contributions from the property company into a pension that then invests in commercial property. This creates a powerful structure where the property company's profits fund pension investments in further property assets.

## Section 24 and pension property

The mortgage interest restriction that affects individual landlords does not apply to SIPPs or SSAS. Borrowing to finance property purchases within a pension remains fully deductible against rental income, regardless of the tax rate.

This is one of the reasons why pension-held property is increasingly popular with higher-rate and additional-rate taxpayers.

## Common pitfalls

- **Buying residential property in a SIPP:** The tax charge of up to 55% makes this a costly mistake.
- **Personal use of the property:** You cannot live in or use property held in your SIPP.
- **Related party transactions:** Buying a property from yourself or a close family member using the SIPP is not permitted.
- **Insufficient funds for costs:** The SIPP must have enough cash to cover ongoing costs like repairs, insurance, and management fees.
- **Concentration risk:** Putting all your pension into one property creates significant risk if the property market drops.

## Final thought

Pensions are a powerful but underused way to invest in commercial property. The tax advantages are significant, and the structure is well-established. However, the rules are specific and the costs of getting it wrong are high.

Professional advice from a pension specialist and a property solicitor is essential before you use a SIPP or SSAS for property investment. The cost of advice is a fraction of what you could lose from a mistake.
