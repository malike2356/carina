# 86 - Property Investment Beginner Foundations

## Purpose
Plain-English explanations of every core concept a complete beginner needs before doing their first deal. Written for Carina to use when answering basic questions and for beginners who have never invested in property before.

---

## What property investment actually is

You buy a property (or control one without buying it) and either collect rent from tenants each month (income strategy) or sell it at a higher price than you paid (capital growth strategy), or both. The goal is to build wealth and replace your job income over time.

You do not need to be a landlord fixing toilets at midnight. Most investors use letting agents and property managers to handle day-to-day issues.

---

## The 7 core UK strategies (plain English)

### Buy to Let (BTL)
You buy a property with a buy-to-let mortgage (not a residential mortgage - different product), rent it to tenants on a 6-12 month assured shorthold tenancy, collect rent monthly, and build equity over time as the mortgage is paid down (if repayment) or as the property rises in value. Simplest strategy. Lowest management burden with a good letting agent.

### BRR (Buy, Refurbish, Refinance, Repeat)
You buy a run-down property at below market value (BMV), refurbish it to increase its value, then refinance it at the new higher value. The refinance releases capital back to you. If done well, you recover most or all of your initial investment and still own the property generating rent. Then you use that recycled capital to buy the next deal. Requires trades connections or project management skills.

### HMO (House in Multiple Occupation)
Instead of one tenant on a single let, you rent each room separately to individual tenants. A 5-bed house might earn £3,000/mo in a HMO vs £900/mo as a single let. Higher yield but more complex: requires a licence (most councils require one for 5+ people), more management, more maintenance, and a specialist HMO mortgage. Article 4 directions in some areas mean you need planning permission too.

### Rent to Rent (R2R)
You do not buy anything. You lease a property from a landlord at a fixed monthly rent (e.g. £800/mo), then sublet it to multiple tenants or on Airbnb for more (e.g. £1,400/mo). You keep the difference (£600/mo) minus bills and running costs. Very low capital to start (£2-5k for setup, first month rent, deposit). Risk: if your tenants leave, you still owe the landlord rent.

### Serviced Accommodation (SA)
Short-term furnished lettings via AirBnb, Booking.com, and corporate relocation companies. A flat that earns £700/mo on a normal tenancy might earn £2,500/mo as SA if in the right location. Requires furnishing the property to a hotel standard, managing check-ins/checkouts, cleaning, and platform listings. A SA management company can handle everything for 20-30% of revenue.

### Property Flip
Buy a distressed property, renovate it quickly, sell it for a profit. No ongoing monthly income - pure capital gain. Faster cycle (4-6 months), but no passive income and you pay capital gains tax on each sale. Works best with trades skills or a trusted builder who gives reliable quotes. Profit per deal can be £15,000-£60,000 depending on deal size.

### Commercial Property
Buy an office, retail unit, warehouse, or mixed-use building and lease it to a business tenant on a long-term lease (3-25 years). Business tenants typically pay their own maintenance costs (Full Repairing and Insuring lease). More stable than residential. Potential for permitted development - converting offices or retail into flats, which can dramatically increase the asset's value.

## The metrics every investor must understand

### Gross Yield
The annual rent as a percentage of the purchase price.
Formula: (annual rent / purchase price) × 100
Example: property costs £150,000, rents for £750/mo.
Annual rent = £9,000. Gross yield = (9,000 / 150,000) × 100 = **6%**

A good gross yield for BTL is 6-8% in the UK. HMO and SA can reach 12-20%.

What it tells you: whether the rent covers the mortgage and basic costs. A rule of thumb: you need gross yield above roughly 1.5× the mortgage rate to make money. At 5.5% mortgage rates, you need at least 7-8% gross yield to generate positive cash flow.

### Net Yield
Gross yield minus all costs (management fees, maintenance allowance, void periods, insurance, letting fees, mortgage interest). More accurate but harder to calculate before purchase. Typically 1-2 percentage points below gross yield.

### Cash Flow
The monthly money left in your pocket after every cost is paid including the mortgage. This is what matters for income investors. A property with positive cash flow puts money into your account every month. Negative cash flow costs you money every month.

Cash flow = monthly rent - mortgage payment - management fee - maintenance allowance - insurance - void allowance

Example: rent £900, mortgage £550, management £90, maintenance £75, insurance £25 = cash flow £160/mo

### Return on Investment (ROI)
The annual profit as a percentage of the cash you put in (not the property value).
Formula: (annual net profit / capital invested) × 100
Example: you invested £55,000 (deposit + costs), annual cash flow = £1,920, annual equity paydown = £1,200 (if repayment mortgage), total return = £3,120. ROI = (3,120 / 55,000) × 100 = **5.7%**

ROI improves as time passes because equity builds and rent increases but your mortgage stays fixed.

### Cash on Cash Return (CoC)
Like ROI but counts only the actual cash income vs cash invested, ignoring equity. More conservative.
Formula: (annual cash flow / cash invested) × 100
Using the above: (1,920 / 55,000) × 100 = **3.5% CoC**

### Loan to Value (LTV)
What percentage of the property value you are borrowing.
Example: £150,000 property, £112,500 mortgage = 75% LTV (you put in 25% deposit).

Buy-to-let mortgages typically require 25% deposit (75% LTV maximum). Higher LTV = smaller deposit needed but higher interest rate and higher monthly mortgage payment = less cash flow.

### DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio)
Monthly rental income divided by monthly mortgage payment. Used by lenders to stress test whether the property pays for itself.
Formula: monthly rent / monthly mortgage
Example: rent £1,000, mortgage £687 = DSCR of 1.45

Most BTL lenders require DSCR of at least 1.25 (rent covers mortgage by 125% or more). If DSCR is below 1.25, the lender usually will not approve the mortgage at that amount.

### Equity
The difference between the property value and the outstanding mortgage.
Example: property worth £200,000, mortgage balance £120,000 = £80,000 equity.

Equity grows in two ways:
1. Mortgage paydown (if you use a repayment mortgage, each payment reduces the balance)
2. Capital appreciation (property value increases)

You cannot spend equity directly. You access it by: selling the property, refinancing (taking out a new mortgage at the current value), or using it as a deposit for another property via a "further advance."

### Void Period
The time between tenants when no rent is received. Industry standard is to budget for 4 weeks void per year (roughly 7-8% of annual rent) as a running cost. In strong rental markets voids can be much lower.

### Yield Belt
The regions of the UK where property prices are lower but rental demand is still strong, producing higher yields than London and the South East. Common yield belt areas: Manchester, Liverpool, Leeds, Birmingham, Sheffield, Nottingham, Cardiff, parts of the East Midlands.

## Common beginner mistakes

**1. Buying near home because it feels safe**
Emotional proximity is not a sound investment criterion. Your best deal might be 200 miles away in a higher-yielding market. Use data: rental yield, vacancy rates, employment levels, transport links.

**2. Calculating yield on asking price instead of purchase price**
Always use the actual price you pay including stamp duty, legal fees, survey, and refurb. These costs add up to 3-8% on top of the purchase price. Your true capital deployed is significantly higher than the mortgage deposit alone.

**3. Underestimating running costs**
Beginners often use just "rent minus mortgage = profit." In reality: management fees (8-12%), maintenance reserve (5-10% of rent), insurance (~£150-400/yr), mortgage arrangement fees amortised, accountancy, void periods, and letting fees all erode income. A property producing £900/mo in rent might net £150-200/mo after everything.

**4. Over-leveraging**
Taking out as many mortgages as possible to buy the maximum number of properties works well when values rise and interest rates fall. When rates rise or values fall, highly leveraged portfolios can quickly become cash-flow negative. Maintain a cash buffer of at least 3 months of mortgage payments per property.

**5. Skipping due diligence on location**
The single biggest driver of future value is location. Jobs, transport, schools, regeneration. A cheap property in an area with declining employment and population shrinkage will not appreciate. Check: local council regeneration plans, major employer presence, university towns, infrastructure investment.

**6. Not understanding tax**
Rental income is taxable as income. Section 24 (2017 rules) means landlords can no longer deduct mortgage interest as a cost - they get a 20% tax credit instead. Higher-rate taxpayers (40%) are significantly affected. Using a limited company for buy-to-let changes the tax treatment but has its own costs (accountancy, corporation tax, dividend tax on withdrawal). Get a specialist property tax accountant before buying.

**7. Treating SA as a passive investment**
Serviced accommodation delivers significantly higher income but it is NOT passive without a management company. Without one, expect to handle check-in/out, cleaning coordination, guest messages at midnight, and maintenance calls seven days a week.

**8. Waiting for the perfect deal**
Analysis paralysis is the number-one reason aspiring investors do nothing for years. A deal that pencils at a 7% gross yield and positive cash flow is a good deal. The market moves; waiting for 10% yields in London will mean waiting forever.

## How much money do I need to start?

**R2R or Rent-to-SA:** £2,000 - £8,000 (setup costs, first month's rent, holding deposit, furniture for SA). This is the lowest entry point.

**BTL (Northern England, Midlands):** £30,000 - £60,000 (25% deposit on a £100,000-£200,000 property, plus stamp duty, legal fees, survey, refurb if needed).

**HMO:** £55,000 - £110,000 (higher deposits because HMO mortgages can be harder, plus conversion costs for rooms, licensing, fire safety).

**BRR:** £70,000 - £120,000 (purchase + full refurb costs upfront before the refinance returns capital).

**SA (purchase):** £35,000 - £70,000 (deposit on cheaper property in a strong SA market, plus furniture package £5,000-£15,000).

**Flip:** £80,000 - £150,000 (purchase price + refurb + holding costs, most returned on sale).

**Commercial:** £120,000+ (higher minimum deposits, lenders require 30-40% for commercial property).

## The power team every investor needs

**Mortgage broker (specialist in investment mortgages):** Not a high street bank. Use a whole-of-market broker who specialises in buy-to-let, HMO, and bridging products. They know which lenders accept which deal types and can get deals not available direct.

**Property solicitor (conveyancer):** Handles the legal transfer of the property. Use one experienced in investment purchases (not just residential). They review the lease (if leasehold), title, searches, and contracts.

**Accountant (property specialist):** Tax planning from day one saves significant money. Advises on company vs personal ownership, VAT on commercial, capital gains strategies.

**Letting agent (area specialist):** Manages tenant finding, referencing, deposit protection, rent collection, inspections, and maintenance. The difference between a good and bad agent is enormous.

**Mortgage/bridging surveyor:** Lenders require a valuation. For BRR and commercial, a RICS surveyor who understands investment value matters.

**Trades team or project manager:** For BRR and flips especially. A trusted builder who quotes accurately and delivers on time is one of the most valuable assets an investor can have.

---

## How long before I see returns?

**R2R / SA (rent-to-SA):** Income can start within 30-60 days of signing a lease agreement.

**SA (purchase):** 2-3 months after exchanging contracts (completion + furnishing + listings going live).

**BTL:** 2-4 months after purchase (legal process + finding tenants + referencing).

**BRR:** 6-9 months (purchase + refurb typically 2-4 months + refinance process 1-2 months + tenant).

**HMO:** 3-5 months (conversion work + licensing + tenant sourcing).

**Flip:** 4-8 months (purchase + refurb + sale legal process).

**Commercial:** 4-6 months (longer legal process + tenant find if vacant).

## The most important mindset truth

Property investment is not a get-rich-quick scheme. The investors who build genuine wealth do so by:
1. Buying well (below market value, in strong areas, at the right yield)
2. Managing costs tightly (knowing their numbers every single month)
3. Holding for the long term (capital growth compounds over 10-20 years)
4. Reinvesting profits (recycling equity and cash flow into more deals)
5. Building a team (never trying to do everything themselves)

The first deal is always the hardest. It takes the most research, the most fear, and the most time. The second deal takes half the time. The tenth deal takes a week.
