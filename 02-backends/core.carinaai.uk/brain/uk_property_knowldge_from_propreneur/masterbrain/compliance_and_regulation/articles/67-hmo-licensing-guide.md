# HMO Licensing: A Complete Guide for Landlords

Houses in Multiple Occupation (HMOs) are one of the most profitable property investment strategies in the UK, but they are also the most regulated. The licensing rules have tightened significantly in recent years, and landlords who do not comply face substantial fines, rent repayment orders, and even prosecution.

This guide covers everything you need to know about HMO licensing in 2026.

## What counts as an HMO

A property is an HMO if it is occupied by three or more people who are not from the same household, and they share facilities such as a kitchen, bathroom, or toilet. This covers shared houses, student lets, and some types of supported accommodation.

Not all HMOs require a licence, but the rules have expanded significantly.

## Mandatory HMO licensing

Since 1 October 2018, mandatory HMO licensing applies to properties that meet all of the following criteria:

- Occupied by five or more people forming two or more separate households
- Shared facilities (kitchen, bathroom, or toilet)
- The property is at least three storeys high
- The property is the occupiers' main residence

The three-storey requirement was a significant restriction. However, from 1 October 2018, the government removed this requirement in England. Since then, mandatory licensing has applied to all HMOs meeting the following criteria:

- Five or more occupiers from two or more households
- Shared facilities
- Used as a main residence

This change brought thousands of previously unlicensed properties into scope.

## Additional and selective licensing

Beyond mandatory licensing, local authorities can operate additional HMO licensing schemes that cover properties with three or four occupiers, or all HMOs in a specific area regardless of size.

Portsmouth City Council operates additional HMO licensing across large parts of the city. If you are buying an HMO in Portsmouth, check whether additional licensing applies to the property. The scheme covers properties occupied by three or more people who are not from the same household.

Selective licensing is a separate scheme that applies to all private rented properties in designated areas, not just HMOs. Some councils use selective licensing to address specific issues in neighbourhoods with high concentrations of private renting.

Always check with the local council before buying an HMO. Do not assume that because a property has not been licensed before, it does not need a licence now.

## The licence conditions

An HMO licence comes with conditions that you must comply with. These typically include:

- The property must be suitable for the number of occupants (adequate room sizes)
- The licence holder must be a fit and proper person
- Minimum floor space requirements for bedrooms
- Adequate kitchen, bathroom, and WC facilities for the number of occupants
- Working fire detection and alarm systems
- Fire doors in specified locations
- Annual gas safety checks and five-yearly electrical installation checks
- Valid EPC with a minimum rating of E (soon to be C)
- Appropriate refuse storage and disposal facilities

Local authorities can add their own conditions. Some require regular inspections, others mandate specific types of fire alarm systems, and many require that the landlord or managing agent completes accredited landlord training.

## The application process

Applying for an HMO licence involves:

1. Submitting the application form to the local council
2. Providing details of the property, the proposed occupiers, and the management arrangements
3. Providing a floor plan showing room sizes
4. Submitting proof of gas safety, electrical safety, and EPC
5. Declaring that the licence holder is a fit and proper person
6. Paying the application fee

Fees vary by council. In Portsmouth, a five-bed HMO licence currently costs approximately GBP 800 to GBP 1,000. Some councils charge per occupant, which can make large HMOs expensive to licence.

The licence is valid for five years. You must renew before it expires.

## Penalties for non-compliance

The penalties for operating an unlicensed HMO are severe:

- Unlimited fine if prosecuted in the magistrates' court
- Rent repayment orders of up to 12 months' rent, applied for by tenants or the council
- Banning orders preventing the landlord from letting property
- Entry on the national database of rogue landlords and property agents
- Prohibition on serving Section 21 notices to regain possession

A single unlicensed HMO can cost a landlord tens of thousands of pounds in rent repayment alone, plus legal costs and the cost of retrospectively applying for a licence.

## Practical advice for HMO investors

- Check the licence position before you buy. Contact the council and ask what scheme applies to the postcode.
- Budget for the licence fee, any required improvements, and ongoing compliance costs.
- Use a solicitor who understands HMO licensing. Not all property solicitors do.
- Keep detailed records of all safety checks, inspections, and communications with the council.
- If you use a managing agent, ensure they are complying with the licensing conditions. The responsibility remains with the licence holder.

## Final thought

HMO licensing is not going away. If anything, the trend is towards tighter regulation across more areas. The best approach is to treat compliance as a standard cost of doing business, build it into your deal analysis, and run a property that meets or exceeds the required standards. A well-run, properly licensed HMO is a strong investment. A poorly run, unlicensed one is a liability.
