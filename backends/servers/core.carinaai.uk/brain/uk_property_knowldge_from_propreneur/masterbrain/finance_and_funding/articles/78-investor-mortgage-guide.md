# The Investor's Guide to Buy-to-Let Mortgages

Buy-to-let mortgages are different from residential mortgages in important ways. The application process, the criteria, and the costs all differ. Understanding these differences is essential before you start building a portfolio.

This guide covers everything you need to know about buy-to-let mortgages in 2026.

## How buy-to-let mortgages differ from residential

The fundamental difference is how lenders assess the application. For a residential mortgage, they look at your income and affordability. For a buy-to-let mortgage, they look at the rental income and the property value.

Key differences:

- Interest rates are typically 0.5% to 1.5% higher than equivalent residential rates
- Most are interest-only, not repayment
- Arrangement fees are usually higher
- The minimum deposit is typically 25%, though 20% is available in some cases
- The lender assesses the rental income against the mortgage payment, not your personal income

## The rental income test

This is the most important calculation in a buy-to-let mortgage application. Lenders require the rental income to cover the mortgage payment by a certain margin.

The standard test is: the annual rent must be at least 125% to 145% of the mortgage payment, calculated at the lender's reversion rate or a stress rate (typically 5.5% to 6% interest).

For example, if you borrow GBP 150,000 on an interest-only basis at a stress rate of 5.5%, the annual mortgage payment is GBP 8,250. The lender wants the annual rent to be at least 125% of that, or GBP 10,312 per year (GBP 859 per month).

If the property in Hampshire rents for GBP 900 per month (GBP 10,800 per year), it passes the test. If it rents for GBP 750 per month, it fails.

Some lenders are more flexible for portfolio landlords and professional investors, applying lower stress rates or different calculations. Specialist lenders may accept a lower cover ratio if the borrower has significant personal income or assets.

## Interest-only vs repayment

Almost all buy-to-let mortgages are interest-only. You pay the interest each month and the capital is repaid when you sell the property or refinance.

This structure works because:

- It keeps monthly payments lower, maximising cash flow
- Capital growth provides the return, not capital repayment
- You can use the cash flow to fund further investments

The risk is that you are relying on the property value increasing to repay the capital. If property prices fall, or if you need to sell in a depressed market, you could end up with less than the outstanding mortgage.

Some investors use repayment mortgages or partial repayment to reduce this risk, accepting lower cash flow in exchange for building equity.

## Limited company buy-to-let mortgages

Since the mortgage interest restriction was introduced in 2017, more investors have set up limited companies to hold their rental properties. Limited company buy-to-let mortgages are available, but they differ from personal mortgages:

- Interest rates are usually 0.5% to 1% higher
- Fees tend to be higher
- Fewer lenders offer them, especially for new companies
- The application process requires full company accounts and tax returns
- Some lenders require the company to have been trading for at least 12 months

However, the tax advantages can outweigh the higher costs. A limited company pays corporation tax on rental profits (19% to 25%) rather than income tax at 40% to 45%, and can deduct mortgage interest in full.

## Portfolio landlord mortgages

If you have four or more buy-to-let properties, you are classified as a portfolio landlord by most lenders. Portfolio lending is more restricted:

- Fewer lenders offer portfolio facilities
- Underwriting is more complex, often requiring a full portfolio review
- The lender assesses the whole portfolio, not just one property
- Interest rates may be higher

Portfolio landlords should use a specialist mortgage broker who understands this market. A good broker can match your portfolio profile with the right lender.

## HMO and multi-unit mortgages

Standard buy-to-let mortgages do not work for HMOs or multi-unit blocks. You need specialist products that account for the different risk profile and income structure.

HMO lenders typically:

- Charge higher rates (0.5% to 2% above standard BTL)
- Require larger deposits (30% to 40%)
- Assess the property on its rental schedule, not a single rental figure
- Want to see the HMO licence and evidence of compliance
- May restrict the number of occupants or rooms

Multi-unit block mortgages (for properties with multiple self-contained units) are a separate product category with their own criteria.

## The application process

The buy-to-let mortgage application process follows these steps:

1. **Preparation:** Check your credit score, gather documents (ID, proof of address, bank statements, tax returns), and prepare a deal pack with expected rental income
2. **Decision in Principle:** Most lenders offer a quick DIP that confirms your borrowing capacity
3. **Full application:** Submit the full application with supporting documents
4. **Valuation:** The lender instructs a valuation to confirm the property value and rental assessment
5. **Offer:** The lender issues a formal mortgage offer
6. **Legal work:** Your solicitor handles the conveyancing and security registration
7. **Completion:** The funds are released and the property purchase completes

The process typically takes 4 to 8 weeks from application to completion, though some lenders offer express services that complete in 2 to 3 weeks.

## Common application mistakes

- Applying without checking your credit score first
- Changing jobs or taking on new credit during the application process
- Not having the deposit funds clearly evidenced
- Over-estimating the rental income
- Choosing the wrong lender or product for your situation
- Not using a specialist buy-to-let broker

## Final thought

The buy-to-let mortgage market has more options than many investors realise. Rates, criteria, and products vary significantly between lenders. A professional mortgage broker who specialises in buy-to-let is worth their fee, often saving you far more than they charge.

The right mortgage structure can make a good deal great. The wrong one can turn a good deal into a break-even exercise.
