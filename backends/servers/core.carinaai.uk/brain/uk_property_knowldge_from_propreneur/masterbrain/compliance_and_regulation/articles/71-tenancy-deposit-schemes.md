# Tenancy Deposit Schemes: Protection Rules Every Landlord Must Follow

If you take a deposit from a tenant in England or Wales, you must protect it in a government-approved tenancy deposit scheme. The rules are clear, the penalties for non-compliance are serious, and this is one area where there is no room for error.

This guide covers how deposit protection works and what landlords need to do.

## The legal requirement

Since 6 April 2007, all deposits taken for assured shorthold tenancies in England and Wales must be protected in one of three government-approved schemes within 30 days of receiving the money.

The three approved schemes are:

- Deposit Protection Service (DPS) - custodial and insurance options
- MyDeposits - insurance-based
- Tenancy Deposit Scheme (TDS) - insurance-based

Custodial schemes hold the deposit money for the duration of the tenancy. Insurance-based schemes allow the landlord to hold the deposit, but the landlord pays a premium and the scheme guarantees the protection.

## The 30-day deadline

Within 30 days of receiving the deposit, you must:

1. Pay the deposit into the scheme (custodial) or register it (insurance)
2. Provide the tenant with the prescribed information: the scheme details, the deposit amount, the property address, your contact details, and information about how disputes are resolved

The 30-day deadline runs from the date you receive the deposit, not from the start of the tenancy. If a tenant pays a holding deposit that is later converted to a tenancy deposit, the deadline runs from when the holding deposit becomes a tenancy deposit.

Missing the 30-day deadline is a breach in itself, even if you protect the deposit later.

## What counts as a deposit

A deposit is any money paid by the tenant to secure the tenancy. This includes:

- A damage deposit
- A holding deposit that is converted to a tenancy deposit
- A guarantor's deposit
- A pet deposit

The rules apply regardless of the amount. Even a GBP 100 deposit must be protected.

## The prescribed information

Alongside protecting the money, you must provide the tenant with:

- The name and contact details of the deposit scheme
- The address of the property
- The amount of deposit paid
- How the deposit is held (custodial or insurance)
- A leaflet or information about the scheme explaining how deposits work and how disputes are resolved
- Your contact details and those of the letting agent (if used)
- Confirmation that the tenant should contact the scheme if there is a dispute at the end of the tenancy
- Signature from both you and the tenant confirming receipt of the information

This information must be provided within 30 days of receiving the deposit.

## What happens if you get it wrong

The penalties for failing to protect a deposit or failing to provide the prescribed information are severe.

If the tenant takes you to court, the judge can order you to:

- Return the deposit to the tenant in full
- Pay the tenant between one and three times the deposit amount as compensation

A GBP 1,000 deposit could result in a penalty of up to GBP 3,000 on top of having to return the deposit. This is a strict liability offence. It does not matter whether the failure was accidental. If you did not protect the deposit on time, you are liable.

Beyond the financial penalty, a tenant can use a deposit protection failure to challenge a Section 21 eviction notice. If you have not protected the deposit and provided the prescribed information, the court will not give you possession of the property if you serve a Section 21 notice. You must fix the breach first, and even then you may have difficulty.

## Serving a Section 21 after a deposit breach

If you have failed to protect a deposit, you must return it to the tenant, or protect it and provide the prescribed information, before you can serve a valid Section 21 notice. The court will not accept a Section 21 if there is an outstanding deposit protection issue.

The practical effect is that you cannot evict a tenant if your deposit paperwork is not in order, even if the tenant has breached their tenancy terms.

## Deductions from deposits

At the end of a tenancy, you can make deductions from the deposit for:

- Unpaid rent
- Damage beyond fair wear and tear
- Cleaning required to return the property to the condition it was in at the start
- Missing items that were listed in the inventory

You cannot make deductions for:

- Normal wear and tear (scuffed paintwork, worn carpets)
- Damage that existed before the tenancy started
- Items that were not listed in the check-in inventory

If there is a dispute about deductions, the deposit scheme offers a free dispute resolution service. Both parties must agree to use it. If either party disagrees, the dispute goes to court.

To make a successful deduction claim, you need evidence:

- An inventory with photographs from check-in and check-out
- Receipts or quotes for repairs
- The tenancy agreement clauses that support the deduction

Without proper evidence, the dispute resolution service will typically award the disputed amount to the tenant.

## Practical tips

- Protect the deposit on the day you receive it, not on day 29
- Use a template for the prescribed information that meets all the legal requirements
- Keep proof of protection (the scheme certificate) and proof of providing the prescribed information
- Take dated photographs at check-in and check-out
- For student HMOs, note that joint tenancy deposits must be protected once, not per tenant
- Get professional advice if the tenancy is complex or the deposit amount is significant

## Final thought

Deposit protection is one of the simplest legal obligations in the private rented sector, and it is one of the most common reasons landlords end up in court. If you take a deposit, protect it within 30 days and provide the prescribed information. It takes ten minutes and it protects your legal position for the whole tenancy.
