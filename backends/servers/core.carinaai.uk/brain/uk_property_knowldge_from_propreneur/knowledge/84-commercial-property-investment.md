# Commercial Property Investment

Carina should use this document to answer questions about commercial property investment strategies, commercial leases, motivated seller identification, and value-add approaches in the UK commercial market.

---

## Market Opportunity in 2026

A generational shift is underway in UK commercial property ownership. Baby Boomers who built commercial portfolios are now reaching the stage where they want to exit. This creates a window of opportunity characterised by:

- Willing sellers who need to exit for lifestyle or age reasons
- High asking prices driven by seller expectation (not market reality)
- An expectation gap between what sellers want and what buyers will pay
- Opportunity to find motivated sellers willing to negotiate meaningfully below asking price

Important: asking prices in commercial are frequently overpriced. A 10% discount from asking price is not necessarily a good deal. Always verify pricing against comparable transactions, not just asking price.

## Return on Investment Benchmarks

| Deal type | Typical ROI range |
|---|---|
| Secure income (long lease, stable tenant) | 10-20% |
| Value-add deals | 25-50%+ |
| Money-out (refinance after forced value) | Infinite ROI - capital returned in 3-6 months |

The quality of the deal and the depth of value-add potential determines where in these ranges a deal sits.

## Finding Motivated Commercial Sellers

The same motivated seller principles that apply in residential apply in commercial. Look for:

- Properties with high asking prices relative to recent comparables
- Owners who have held long-term and whose circumstances have changed
- Generational exits (estate sales, retirement)
- Vacant units where the owner is holding an empty asset
- Properties where the tenant is not using all the space (e.g. storage above a shop lying empty)

Finding motivated sellers in commercial often comes through:
- Local commercial agents
- Direct outreach to landlords of vacant or underused properties
- Networking at property investor communities

## Commercial Leases: Key Concepts

**Lease term:** The contracted period a tenant agrees to occupy and pay rent. Common terms: 3, 5, 10, 25 years.

**Break clause:** A clause within a lease that allows the tenant (or sometimes the landlord) to exit the lease before the end of the contracted term, subject to notice. Break clauses are negotiated at the point of signing. In some markets, break clauses are standard; in others they require negotiation. Always check when break clauses fall due on an acquisition - they represent a risk of income interruption.

**Inside vs outside the Act:** Tenants holding commercial leases that are "inside" the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954 have statutory rights to renew their lease at the end of the term. Tenants "outside" the Act have no automatic right to renew. At the end of a lease, "inside the Act" tenants cannot simply be asked to leave - the landlord must follow a specific legal process. Understanding this is essential before any commercial acquisition.

**Surrender:** A tenant hands their lease back to the landlord before the end of the contracted term. Both parties must agree.

**Part surrender:** A tenant surrenders only part of the leased premises (e.g. the upper floor or storage area they are not using), allowing the landlord to access that space for alternative use.

**Service charge:** Commercial tenants commonly pay a service charge covering maintenance of common areas, building insurance, and shared services, in addition to base rent.

## Value-Add Commercial Strategies

**Retail with residential above (mixed use):**
- Common in high streets: shop below, potential flats or storage above
- If the upper floor is unused and the tenant occupies the whole building on one lease, negotiate a part surrender to access the upper space
- Always underwrite the deal on its commercial merits first (yield, lease terms, alternative commercial uses for upper floor such as office, beauty salon) - residential conversion of the uppers is the upside, not the primary underwrite
- This provides a fallback if planning permission for residential is not granted

**Planning uplift:** Change of use (commercial to residential, subdivision) can create significant forced value. Assess whether permitted development rights apply.

**Lease restructuring:** Buying a property with a short lease remaining at a high rent, then renegotiating terms, can rebase the property value.

**Adding tenants to vacant units:** A property with vacant units bought at a vacant yield becomes more valuable once tenanted. The margin between vacant and tenanted yields drives returns.

## Commercial Property in Scotland

The legal framework in Scotland differs from England and Wales in some aspects (different contract law, different landlord and tenant statutes). The core investment principles - motivated sellers, value-add, yield analysis - are identical. Specialist Scottish commercial solicitors are required for any transaction.

## When Commercial Makes Sense

Commercial is worth considering when:
- The investor wants genuinely passive, long-term income from institutional-style tenants on long leases
- There is scope to force value through planning, refurbishment, or lease restructuring
- The investor has capital to access the minimum entry price (commercial thresholds are generally higher than residential in prime areas)
- The target ROI is 10%+ on income alone, with capital growth from value-add as the secondary driver

Commercial is not suited to:
- Investors expecting to buy and immediately generate high income without a value-creation phase
- Anyone unwilling to learn the basic lease framework before committing capital
