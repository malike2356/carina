# Leasehold vs Freehold: What Property Investors Need to Know

The distinction between leasehold and freehold ownership is one of the most important concepts for UK property investors to understand. Getting it wrong can mean unexpected costs, legal restrictions, and reduced resale value. This article explains the key differences, the financial implications, and when each tenure type makes sense for your investment strategy.

## Freehold: Complete Ownership

Freehold means you own both the building and the land it sits on, outright and indefinitely. There is no ground rent, no service charge, and no landlord to answer to. You control the property completely - from structural repairs to exterior decoration.

For a property investor, freehold is generally the preferred tenure for houses. A freehold terraced house in Southsea or a freehold bungalow on the Isle of Wight gives you maximum flexibility. You can extend, convert, or renovate subject only to planning permission and building regulations. There is no superior landlord to seek consent from, and no expiry date on your ownership.

The main drawback is that all maintenance and repair costs fall on you entirely - there is no service charge to cover shared elements because there are none.

## Leasehold: Ownership for a Term

Leasehold means you own the property for a fixed period - the lease term - but not the land it stands on. The land belongs to the freeholder (also called the landlord). Most flats and apartments in England and Wales are leasehold. You will also find leasehold houses, particularly in newer developments.

When you buy a leasehold property, you effectively purchase the right to occupy and use the property for the remaining term of the lease. Leases are typically granted for 99, 125, or 999 years. The shorter the remaining term, the more it affects the property's value and your ability to mortgage it.

## Key Financial Obligations for Leaseholders

**Ground rent.** An annual payment to the freeholder. Historically ground rent was nominal - often £50-£100 per year. However, many modern leases include escalating ground rent provisions that double every 10 or 25 years. These onerous clauses can seriously reduce a property's saleability and have attracted regulatory attention. The Leasehold Reform (Ground Rent) Act 2022 now prohibits ground rent on most new residential long leases.

**Service charge.** Covers the cost of maintaining common parts - hallways, lifts, gardens, roofs, and structural elements of the building. Service charges vary enormously. In a modern Portsmouth apartment block with lifts and concierge, you might pay £2,000-£4,000 per year. In a small converted Victorian house, a few hundred pounds may suffice. Always review the service charge history and any planned major works before purchasing.

**Building insurance.** The freeholder arranges building insurance and passes the cost through the service charge. As a leaseholder, you only need contents insurance.

## What Happens as the Lease Runs Down

Leases depreciate as they get shorter. When a lease falls below 80 years remaining, the property becomes harder to mortgage and less attractive to buyers. Below 70 years, many lenders will not lend at all.

The solution is lease extension. Under the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993, qualifying leaseholders have the right to extend their lease by 90 years (for flats) at a peppercorn ground rent. The cost depends on the property value, the remaining lease term, and the ground rent. As a rough guide, extending a lease from 85 years on a Portsmouth flat worth £200,000 might cost £8,000-£15,000 including legal and valuation fees.

**The 80-year trap.** Once the lease falls below 80 years, the cost of extension increases significantly because marriage value becomes payable to the freeholder. For this reason, investors should avoid buying properties with leases below 85 years unless the extension cost has been factored into the purchase price.

## Enfranchisement: Buying the Freehold

Collective enfranchisement allows leaseholders in a building to club together and buy the freehold. This gives you control over the building, eliminates ground rent, and removes the need for future lease extensions. For investors who own multiple flats in the same building, buying the freehold can be an excellent long-term play.

The process requires at least half of the leaseholders to participate (for collective enfranchisement under the 1993 Act) and involves serving a formal notice on the freeholder. The cost includes the freehold premium plus legal and valuation fees. For a small converted Victorian house in Southsea with three flats, buying the freehold might cost £15,000-£30,000 depending on the ground rent income and lease terms.

## Which Tenure Is Right for Your Investment

For single houses, freehold is almost always preferable. No ground rent, no service charge, and full control over the asset.

For flats, leasehold is the norm and perfectly acceptable provided the lease term is long (ideally 125 years or more), the ground rent is low or peppercorn, and the service charges are reasonable.

For portfolio landlords, buying the freehold of purpose-built blocks or conversions can create a valuable income stream from ground rents and service charge administration, while also protecting the value of your leasehold interests.
