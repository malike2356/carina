# Bridging Loans Explained: Short-Term Finance for Property Investors

Bridging loans are one of the most versatile financial tools available to property investors. They provide short-term funding that bridges a gap, typically between buying a property and securing longer-term finance.

This guide explains how bridging loans work, when they make sense, and what to watch out for.

## What is a bridging loan

A bridging loan is a short-term secured loan, typically lasting between one and 24 months. It is secured against property, either the one you are buying or another property you own. The loan is repaid when you sell the property, refinance onto a longer-term mortgage, or receive other expected funds.

Bridging loans are more expensive than standard mortgages because they are short-term, higher risk, and faster to arrange. The interest rates are typically 0.5% to 1.5% per month, compared to 4% to 6% per year for a buy-to-let mortgage.

## When to use a bridging loan

### Buying at auction

Auction purchases typically require completion within 28 days. Most mortgage lenders cannot process an application that quickly, and mortgages rarely complete in time. A bridging loan provides the funds to complete the purchase, giving you time to arrange a mortgage or carry out refurbishment before refinancing.

If you buy a property at auction in Portsmouth for GBP 200,000, the deposit (10%) is due immediately and completion must happen within four weeks. A bridging loan can fund the purchase while you refurbish the property and arrange a buy-to-let mortgage.

### Buying below market value

Motivated sellers sometimes want a quick completion. If you find a BMV deal but cannot exchange quickly enough for a mortgage, a bridging loan lets you move fast. You pay more for the finance, but you secure the discount.

A property valued at GBP 300,000 offered at GBP 210,000 for a quick completion could leave you GBP 90,000 of equity even after financing costs, provided the bridging loan is managed correctly.

### Refurbishment projects

If you are buying a property that needs significant renovation, a standard mortgage may not be available because the property is uninhabitable. A bridging loan can fund both the purchase and the refurbishment, often through a single facility with a retention released on completion of the works.

A typical structure is: the lender advances 70% of the refurbished value of the property. You use part of this to buy the property and the remainder is held back and released in stages as the refurbishment progresses.

### Chain breaks and delays

If you are selling a property and the buyer's purchase falls through, but you have already committed to buying another property, a bridging loan can fund the purchase while you find a new buyer.

## How much does bridging finance cost

The costs of a bridging loan go beyond the headline interest rate:

- **Arrangement fee:** 1% to 2% of the loan amount, typically added to the loan
- **Valuation fee:** GBP 500 to GBP 1,500 depending on property value
- **Legal fees:** Usually GBP 500 to GBP 1,500, sometimes added to the loan
- **Exit fee:** Some lenders charge 0.5% to 1% of the loan on repayment
- **Interest:** The main cost, typically charged monthly

Total costs for a six-month bridging loan of GBP 200,000 at 0.75% per month would be approximately:

- Interest: GBP 9,000 over six months
- Arrangement fee: GBP 3,000
- Valuation and legal: GBP 2,000
- Total: approximately GBP 14,000

This is expensive, but if the deal works, the cost is justified by the profit.

## Interest options

### Retained interest

The lender deducts the total interest for the loan term from the advance. You receive a smaller amount upfront but do not have to make monthly payments.

### Monthly interest

You pay interest each month. This works better if you have cash flow to cover the payments, but it reduces the total amount you need to repay at the end.

### Rolled-up interest

Interest is added to the loan balance each month and repaid at the exit. The loan grows over time. This is common for refurbishment projects where cash flow is tied up in the works.

## What lenders look for

Bridging lenders focus on the exit strategy, not your income. They want to know how and when the loan will be repaid.

A credible exit strategy is:

- A mortgage offer in principle from a lender
- A new build off-plan sale due to complete
- A sale instruction with an agreed price
- A documented refinance plan

Without a clear exit, most bridging lenders will not lend, or they will charge higher rates to compensate for the risk.

The loan-to-value ratio for bridging is typically 60% to 75% of the property value, either current value or estimated refurbished value (ERV).

## Risks to be aware of

- **Cost escalation:** If the refurbishment takes longer than expected, interest costs eat into the profit. A three-month delay on a GBP 200,000 loan at 0.75% per month costs an extra GBP 4,500.
- **Exit risk:** If the property does not sell or you cannot refinance at the planned point, you may have to extend the loan at additional cost or sell at a discount to repay it.
- **Property value risk:** If the market falls during the loan term, the LTV may exceed the lender's maximum, making refinancing difficult.
- **Early repayment charges:** Some lenders charge a fee if you repay the loan early. Always check the terms.

## Final thought

Bridging loans are a tool, not a strategy. They allow you to move quickly on deals that would otherwise be inaccessible. The key is having a clear, realistic exit plan and building in a margin of safety for delays and cost overruns.

Used wisely, bridging finance can accelerate your portfolio growth. Used carelessly, it can destroy the profit on a deal.
